Every number in the audio chain, where it comes from, and the arithmetic to justify it out loud.

The chain

Six numbers turn half a second of air into a picture

The microphone measures air pressure. Everything after that is arithmetic. This is the whole path, and each box is a parameter an examiner can ask you to defend.

Parameters

Why each number is what it is

These live in src/common/constants.py. If you can say the "why" line for each, you can survive any question about the front end of your pipeline.

AUDIO_SAMPLE_RATE16 000 Hz

Why: Nyquist's theorem says a sample rate of R can represent frequencies up to R/2. At 16 kHz that ceiling is 8 kHz, which covers speech and almost all environmental sound energy. Music CDs use 44.1 kHz because music has content above 8 kHz — engines, barks and sirens do not. Choosing 16 kHz throws away nothing useful and cuts data by 64% versus CD rate.

Nyquist ceiling = 16000 / 2 = 8000 Hz CD rate 44100 Hz → 1 s = 88 200 bytes (int16) Ours 16000 Hz → 1 s = 32 000 bytes (64% less)
AUDIO_CHUNK_DURATION0.5 s

Why: long enough to contain a recognisable sound event (a bark, a horn, a keystroke burst), short enough that the device reacts quickly and each payload stays small. It also sets the labelling assumption: the camera frame is grabbed inside this window, so image and sound describe the same half-second.

0.5 s × 16000 Hz = 8000 samples 8000 × 2 bytes (int16) = 16 000 bytes = 15.6 KB per clip
STFT_WINDOW_LENGTH400 = 25 ms

Why: the standard speech-processing window. Sound changes constantly, so you cannot analyse half a second at once. Over 25 ms a signal is roughly stationary — steady enough to measure its pitches — while still being short enough to catch fast changes. Shorter windows blur pitch; longer ones blur time.

400 samples ÷ 16000 Hz = 0.025 s = 25 ms frequency resolution = 16000 / 512 = 31.25 Hz per bin
STFT_HOP_LENGTH160 = 10 ms

Why: how far the window slides each step. At 10 ms with a 25 ms window, consecutive windows overlap by 15 ms — 60% overlap. Overlap matters: without it, a sound landing on a window boundary would be split and smeared. This is the other half of the standard 25/10 pairing.

160 ÷ 16000 = 0.010 s = 10 ms overlap = 25 − 10 = 15 ms (60% of the window) frames = 1 + 8000/160 = 51
STFT_N_FFT512

Why: the FFT algorithm is fastest when its size is a power of two, and it must be at least as long as the window. The window is 400 samples, so 512 is simply the next power of two up. Using 256 would truncate the window; using 1024 would waste computation on a Raspberry Pi.

powers of two: 256 < 400 ✗ 512 ≥ 400 ✓ 1024 wasteful output bins = 512/2 + 1 = 257 frequency bins
STFT_N_MELS128

Why: the FFT gives 257 evenly-spaced frequency bins, but human hearing is not evenly spaced — we discriminate far better at low frequencies than high. The mel scale squeezes those 257 linear bins into 128 perceptually-spaced bands: fine detail low down, coarser up top. Fewer numbers and a better-matched representation.

257 linear FFT bins → 128 mel bands final shape = (51 frames, 128 mel bands)

Why it matters downstream

These numbers decide your bandwidth result

Strategy A ships the 8000 raw samples. Strategy B ships the 51×128 spectrogram instead. That single swap is the entire difference between them, and the size difference is arithmetic you can do on a whiteboard.

Per 0.5 s clipCalculationSize
Raw audio (Strategy A)8000 × 2 bytes15.6 KB
Spectrogram, float16 (Strategy B)51 × 128 × 2 bytes12.8 KB
Model weights (Strategy C)110 596 params, per round405 KB
The point about Strategy C. A and B cost per sample — twice the data, twice the bytes. C costs per round, no matter how much audio the device holds. That is why C wins as a deployment grows, and it is the strongest single argument in your comparison.

Drill

Questions an examiner will actually ask

Answer out loud first, then open the card. If your answer had the same numbers in it, you know this section.

Why 16 kHz and not 44.1 kHz?

Nyquist: a rate of R captures frequencies up to R/2. 16 kHz captures up to 8 kHz, which covers speech and environmental sound. Music needs 44.1 kHz because it has real content above 8 kHz; engines and barks do not.

"16 kHz gives me an 8 kHz ceiling, which is everything I need for environmental audio, and it costs 64% fewer bytes than CD rate — which matters when the bandwidth of each strategy is the thing I am measuring."

Where does 8000 come from?

0.5 seconds × 16 000 samples per second = 8 000 samples. It is the sample rate multiplied by the clip duration, nothing more.

Why is the spectrogram 51 frames and not 50?

8000 ÷ 160 = 50 hops, and you get one frame at the start before any hop happens — so 1 + 8000/160 = 51. Librosa pads the signal at both ends (center=True) so the first and last frames are still centred on real audio.

"Fifty hops, fifty-one frames — the fencepost."

Why a 25 ms window with a 10 ms hop?

25 ms is short enough that the signal is quasi-stationary — steady enough to measure its frequency content — but long enough to resolve pitch. The 10 ms hop gives 60% overlap so a sound falling on a window boundary is not smeared or missed. It is the standard pairing in speech processing.

The trade-off is the uncertainty principle: a longer window buys frequency precision and loses time precision. 25/10 is the accepted compromise.

Why is n_fft 512 when the window is only 400?

The FFT is fastest at powers of two and cannot be shorter than the window. 400 sits between 256 and 512, so 512 is the smallest legal power of two. The extra 112 samples are zero-padded.

Why 128 mel bands instead of the 257 FFT bins?

Two reasons. First, human hearing resolves low frequencies far better than high ones, and the mel scale matches that — so the representation carries information where it matters. Second, 128 < 257, so it is also a compression: fewer inputs, smaller model, less to transmit.

Why half a second, not two seconds?

Three constraints meet here. It must be long enough to contain a sound event; short enough that the system responds quickly; and short enough that the payload stays small, since per-sample bandwidth is exactly what the strategy comparison measures. It also bounds the labelling assumption — the camera frame sits inside that window, so the picture and the sound describe the same moment.

Your mic records at 44.1 kHz. Isn't that a mismatch?

Yes, and it is handled explicitly. The USB microphone's native rate is 44 100 Hz, so every chunk is resampled to 16 000 Hz with librosa before anything else touches it (MicRecorder.finish). Downsampling discards content above 8 kHz — which is exactly what the 16 kHz decision already accepted.

"The hardware gives me 44.1 kHz; I resample to 16 kHz on the device so the live pipeline and the UrbanSound8K training data live in the same feature space."
